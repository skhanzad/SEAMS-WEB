Coverage, not accuracy

Blind-spot overlap bounds the horizon of multi-agent verification

When AI agents check one another's work, the usual fix for unreliability is to add another checker. This course works through a paper arguing that the extra checker pays off only when it can see what the others miss. It covers every definition, theorem and equation in order, leaves the proofs to the paper, and pairs each idea with a simulation you can run.

Introduction · §1–2

Why adding agents is a bet

The paper's question, its one central curve, and where the idea comes from.

The question

§1§1.1 Summary of contributions

When a team of agents is unreliable, the reflex is to add another agent. A second reviewer, a debate round, a larger jury, a re-check before release: each is the same bet, that independent opinions accumulate into confidence.

Agent teams built from language models make this bet constantly. Planner, coder, debugger and reviewer inspect one another's artifacts, and the pipeline is trusted because several agents agreed.

VocabularyAn artifact is whatever the team produces: a plan, a program, a report. A defect is a real violation of its specification.

The record is contradictory. Ensembles and debate sometimes help a lot and sometimes not at all. The value of multi-agent structure varies with model, task and architecture. Failure taxonomies place many breakdowns in verification and hand-off rather than in an isolated model call.

The oldest warning is also the sharpest. In 1986, Knight and Leveson had programmers independently write versions of the same program from one specification. The versions failed on the same inputs far more often than independence would predict. Checkers that are built separately can still share blind spots.

The paper treats this as an organisational question: who watches what, how much the agents' fields of view overlap, and how a team should be composed. It turns that into a measurement by treating the population of verifying agents as a statistical object and asking what any protocol built on it can guarantee, however the protocol is designed.

One curve

The answer turns on a single curve. Let a defect be described by a hidden state \(\theta\) that captures which defect occurred and how visible it is. Let \(\Delta(\theta)\) be the probability that a randomly drawn agent's judgement changes depending on whether the defect is present. The population's miss curve is

\[ M(k)=\E_\theta\bigl[(1-\Delta(\theta))^{k}\bigr], \]

the chance that \(k\) independent judgements are all uninformative. No protocol spending \(k\) judgements on a defect can achieve a detection rate minus false-alarm rate above \(1-M(k)\), whatever it does with them. Its doubling rate

\[ a(k)=\log_2\frac{M(k)}{M(2k)} \]

measures how much doubling the team improves coverage. The paper calls it the coverage exponent, and it is the quantity that matters.

What the paper shows

  • HorizonIf each stage of a pipeline introduces a defect with probability \(\beta\), risk stays below \(\varepsilon\) only up to depth \(n^*\approx\varepsilon/(\beta M(k))\). When \(M(k)\) decays like \(k^{-a}\), safe depth grows like \(k^{a}\), and verifying a task of depth \(n\) costs \(\Theta(n^{1+1/a})\) judgements. A blind-spot mass \(\pi_0\) caps the depth near \(\varepsilon/(\beta\pi_0)\) for any team.
  • AccuracyTwo populations can share the same average per-agent detection rate yet support arbitrarily different safe depths. Mean verifier accuracy says nothing about what a pool can certify.
  • Transition at a = 1Above \(a=1\), each added agent buys more horizon than the last, and re-reviewing everything can keep a pipeline clean forever. Below it, neither works, and no team size moves a population across the line.
  • MeasuringCalibrating with \(k\) agents pins down \(M(1),\dots,M(k)\) and nothing beyond. No finite calibration can establish that a blind spot exists, though its size can be bounded from above.
  • ChoosingPick teams for coverage \(\Lambda(S)=\log 1/M(S)\), not individual accuracy. Keeping the \(k\) most accurate agents can be exactly \(k\) times worse.
  • EvidenceTwo executable testbeds reproduce every prediction. Populations matched on per-agent accuracy differ in horizon by 15×.

Part I · §3

The model: populations, judgements, horizons

Four objects carry the whole paper: defects with a hidden state, agents drawn from a population, the miss curve, and pipelines. The key fact is that the miss curve is a sequence of moments.

Defects, agents and discriminative power

§3.1Eq. (1)

A defect is a real violation of the specification, introduced at some stage of work. Which defect occurred, and everything about the world that decides how visible it is, is summarised by a hidden variable \(\theta\) in a set \(\Theta\). Nature draws \(\theta\) from a distribution \(\mu\), fixed by the generator and the environment.

ExampleIn the paper's plan-verification testbed, \(\theta\) says which requirement a slip violates. In the program testbed, it says which mutation was introduced.

A verifier agent \(v\) is any procedure that emits a binary judgement about an artifact: FLAG or PASS. Agents are drawn from a design distribution \(\cD\) over prompts, personas, scopes, test-input strategies, random seeds, or whole models. Given \(\theta\), agent \(v\) flags the defective artifact with probability \(q_v(\theta)\) and its corrected counterpart with probability \(p_v(\theta)\), independently across repeated judgements. Averaging over the agents you might draw:

\[ \class{t-1}{Q(\theta)}=\class{t-4}{\E_{v\sim\cD}}\,q_v(\theta),\qquad \class{t-2}{P(\theta)}=\E_{v\sim\cD}\,p_v(\theta),\qquad \class{t-3}{\Delta=|Q-P|} \](1)
\(Q(\theta)\)
Flag rate on the defective artifact, averaged over agents. A random agent's chance of catching this defect.
\(P(\theta)\)
Flag rate on the corrected artifact. A random agent's false-alarm rate on the fixed version.
\(\Delta=|Q-P|\)
Discriminative power at \(\theta\): how much the presence of the defect moves one random agent's verdict.
\(\E_{v\sim\cD}\)
Average over the design distribution, that is, over which agent you happen to draw.

Why the difference rather than \(Q\) alone? An agent that flags 30% of artifacts no matter what has \(Q=P=0.3\) and \(\Delta=0\). It is busy, but its flags carry no information about this defect. \(\Delta\) measures information, not activity. When agents are sound, meaning they never flag correct work (\(p_v\equiv 0\)), \(\Delta=Q\).

Notice that \(\Delta\) is a function of \(\theta\). The same population can see some defects easily and others hardly at all. That spread across defects, not the average, will decide how fast a team's misses fall.

Check yourself
An agent flags 40% of defective artifacts and 40% of their corrected versions. How much does it contribute to \(\Delta\)?
Nothing. Its verdict does not depend on whether the defect is present, so \(q_v-p_v=0\). Its flags are noise.
Can one population have very different \(\Delta(\theta)\) for different defects?
Yes, and that variation is the point. In Figure 1 a requirement covered by many templates has high \(\Delta\), and one covered by none has \(\Delta=0\).

A random team behaves like one coin flipped \(k\) times

§3.1Lemma 3.1
Lemma 3.1 Exchangeable reduction

If a team consists of \(k\) agents drawn i.i.d. from \(\cD\), then conditional on \(\theta\) their judgements are i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Bernoulli}(Q(\theta))\) on the defective artifact and i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Bernoulli}(P(\theta))\) on the corrected one. Conversely, any infinite exchangeable population of binary judgements admits this representation.

In words: once you know which defect you are looking at, a randomly drawn team of \(k\) agents behaves like \(k\) flips of a single coin whose bias is \(Q(\theta)\). Which agents exactly were drawn no longer matters. Randomness enters in two stages: nature picks the defect, then the coin is flipped \(k\) times.

This has a consequence that drives the whole paper. Verdicts are independent given \(\theta\), but not independent overall. If agent 1 missed a defect, that is evidence the defect is a hard one, so agent 2 is more likely to miss it too. For sound agents the excess is exactly a variance across defects:

\[ \Pr[\text{both miss}]-\Pr[\text{miss}]^2 \;=\; M(2)-M(1)^2 \;=\; \operatorname{Var}\bigl(1-\Delta(\theta)\bigr)\;\ge 0. \]

Agents fail together exactly as much as defects differ in visibility. This is Eckhardt and Lee's difficulty-function argument from software reliability, moved from program versions to verifiers.

ExchangeableA sequence is exchangeable if relabelling its members never changes the joint statistics. Agents drawn at random from one design distribution are exchangeable by construction.

The converse direction is de Finetti's theorem. It means the representation is not a modelling assumption about how agents work: any exchangeable population of verdicts looks like this. Nothing here assumes agents are accurate, honest, distinct, or differently built. 240 copies of one agent with different seeds is a legitimate \(\cD\), and so is a mixture of humans and programs.

Check yourself
Two agents drawn from the same population judge the same defect. Are their verdicts independent?
Given the defect, yes. Across defects, no: they are positively correlated, because both depend on how visible the defect is. The covariance of their misses is \(\operatorname{Var}(1-\Delta)\).
When would two random agents' misses be uncorrelated?
When every defect is equally visible (\(\Delta\) constant). Then \(\operatorname{Var}(1-\Delta)=0\) and \(M(2)=M(1)^2\).

The miss curve, the blind spot and the coverage exponent

§3.1Definition 3.2Eqs. (2)–(3)
Definition 3.2 Miss curve, blind-spot mass, coverage exponent
\[ \class{t-1}{M(k)}=\class{t-3}{\E_\theta}\bigl[\class{t-2}{(1-\Delta(\theta))^{k}}\bigr],\qquad \class{t-4}{\pi_0=\Pr[\Delta(\theta)=0]} \](2)
\((1-\Delta(\theta))^{k}\)
For one defect: the chance that \(k\) independent judgements are all unmoved by it. For sound agents, the chance all \(k\) miss it.
\(\E_\theta[\,\cdot\,]\)
Average over which defect occurred, \(\theta\sim\mu\).
\(M(k)\)
The miss curve: the chance a random defect slips past \(k\) random agents.
\(\pi_0\)
The blind-spot mass: the share of defects no agent in the population can ever see.
\[ \class{t-1}{a(k)}=\class{t-2}{\log_2}\class{t-3}{\frac{M(k)}{M(2k)}},\qquad \class{t-4}{\Lambda(k)=\log\frac{1}{M(k)}} \](3)
\(\dfrac{M(k)}{M(2k)}\)
How many times fewer defects slip through when the team doubles from \(k\) to \(2k\).
\(\log_2\)
Turns that factor into an exponent, so that \(M(2k)=M(k)/2^{a(k)}\).
\(a(k)\)

\(\Lambda(k)\)
Coverage on a log scale. The safe pipeline depth is proportional to \(1/M(k)=e^{\Lambda(k)}\).

We write \(M_Q(k)=\E[(1-Q)^k]\) for the miss rate of the "flag if anyone flags" rule, and \(M_P\) for its false-alarm analogue. When agents are sound (\(p_v\equiv0\)) the two coincide, \(M=M_Q\).

The single fact behind the whole theory: \(M(k)\) is the \(k\)-th moment of the random variable \(U=1-\Delta\), which lives in \([0,1]\). Every later result is a statement about moment sequences.

Reading a(k)\(a=1\): doubling the team halves the misses. \(a=2\): it quarters them. \(a\) near 0: doubling barely helps. Since horizon \(\propto 1/M\), doubling multiplies the safe depth by \(2^{a}\).

For large \(k\), \((1-\Delta)^k\) is tiny unless \(\Delta\) is close to 0. So the large-team miss rate is decided by the defects that are hardest to see. The tail of the distribution of \(\Delta\) near zero sets the curve, not its mean. If some defects have \(\Delta=0\) exactly, \(M(k)\) can never fall below their share \(\pi_0\).

The figure below parametrises a population by three numbers. Per-agent accuracy is \(\E[\Delta]\). The blind-spot mass is \(\pi_0\). The tail exponent \(a\) controls how much mass sits near \(\Delta=0\): the share of defects with \(\Delta\le t\) behaves like \(t^{a}\) for small \(t\). At \(a=\infty\) every defect is equally visible; as \(a\to0\) defects become either obvious or invisible. (Technically, \(\Delta\) is a Beta distribution with an optional atom at zero, the same family the paper uses to prove Theorem 4.5.)

Check yourself
\(M(4)=0.10\) and \(M(8)=0.025\). What is \(a(4)\), and what does it buy?
\(a(4)=\log_2(0.10/0.025)=\log_2 4=2\). Going from 4 to 8 agents divides misses by 4, so the safe depth grows about 4×.
A population has \(\pi_0=0.05\). What can you say about \(M(1000)\)?
\(M(1000)\ge0.05\). The curve decreases to \(\pi_0\) and never goes below it.
Why can't the average accuracy \(\E[\Delta]\) predict \(M(64)\)?
\(M(64)=\E[(1-\Delta)^{64}]\) depends almost entirely on the defects with \(\Delta\) below about \(1/64\). The mean barely constrains how much mass sits there.

Pipelines and three assumptions

§3.2

A pipeline of depth \(n\) is a sequence of stages, each writing one component of the artifact. Conditional on any history, stage \(t\) introduces a defect with probability \(\beta\), whose hidden state is drawn independently from \(\mu\). Stages write disjoint components. Between stages, verifying agents may be queried, and if a defect is reported a repair agent may act.

Risk budget\(\varepsilon=0.05\) means you accept a 5% chance that the final artifact is defective.

Part II · §4

What verification can buy

One ceiling bounds every protocol. It converts into a law for how deep a pipeline can go, with sharp consequences for team size, accuracy, re-review and false alarms.

No protocol can beat the miss curve

§4.1Theorem 4.1Eq. (4)

Protocol designers have enormous freedom: majority vote, unanimity, weighted juries, sequential escalation, multi-round debate, re-asking one agent at a higher temperature. All of it is bounded by one curve.

Theorem 4.1 Scrutiny bound

Let a verification protocol observe at most \(k\) agent judgements about a component (choosing adaptively whom to ask and when to stop, and aggregating them arbitrarily and randomly) and output FLAG or PASS. Let \(\delta\) be its flag probability when the defect is present and \(\phi\) when it is absent. Then

\[ \class{t-1}{\delta}-\class{t-2}{\phi}\;\le\;\class{t-3}{1-M(k)}. \](4)
\(\delta\)
Detection rate: the protocol flags when the defect is there.
\(\phi\)
False-alarm rate: the protocol flags when it is not.
\(\delta-\phi\)
Advantage: genuine discrimination. A protocol that always flags has \(\delta=\phi=1\) and advantage 0.
\(1-M(k)\)
The ceiling: the chance that at least one of \(k\) judgements is moved by the defect.

For a heterogeneous pool the same holds with \(M(k)\) replaced by \(\E_\theta\bigl[\min_{|S|\le k}\prod_{i\in S}(1-\Delta_i(\theta))\bigr]\). If agents are sound and the rule is "flag if anyone flags", (4) holds with equality.

Read it as: a protocol can react to a defect only through judgements the defect actually moved. If none of the \(k\) judgements was moved, the protocol sees the same evidence whether or not the defect exists, so it cannot behave differently. More conversation cannot substitute for coverage: debate reshuffles \(k\) judgements, it does not create new ones.

Why it holds (intuition, not the proof)

Picture two worlds that are identical except that one contains the defect. Pair up each agent's verdict in the two worlds so they agree whenever possible; they then disagree with probability \(\Delta(\theta)\), independently across judgements. Run the protocol in both worlds side by side. Until the first judgement that differs, it asks the same agents, sees the same answers and makes the same choices. So the chance it ends up acting differently is at most the chance that some judgement differs, \(1-(1-\Delta)^k\). Averaging over \(\theta\) gives \(1-M(k)\).

The one hypothesis that is not free is conditional independence, and seeing why shows where the leverage is. Agents that are negatively correlated given \(\theta\), in the sense of Littlewood and Miller's forced diversity, can push the chance that all \(k\) agree below \(\E[(1-\Delta)^k]\), and then the bound does not apply. Sampling agents at random from a design distribution never does this. Choosing agents can. Selection (Part IV) is the constructive counterpart of this theorem, and the negative correlation it manufactures is its whole value.

The heterogeneous version handles a pool of distinct agents with their own \(\Delta_i(\theta)\). For each defect, the best a protocol could hope for is to have asked the \(k\) agents least likely to miss it together, which is the minimum over subsets.

Check yourself
Five agents each give a verdict, then argue for two rounds and vote. Nobody re-examines the artifact. Can the debate's detection minus false alarms exceed \(1-M(5)\)?
No. The later rounds are functions of the same five judgements (plus randomness), so the protocol uses at most five judgements and Theorem 4.1 applies. Only new judgements, not more talk, raise the ceiling.
Which protocol attains the bound exactly?
With sound agents, "flag if anyone flags": it never false-alarms (\(\phi=0\)) and misses only when all \(k\) miss, so \(\delta=1-M(k)\).

The shape every miss curve must have

§4.1Proposition 4.2
Proposition 4.2 Shape of the miss curve

\(M\) is completely monotone,

\[ \class{t-1}{\sum_{i=0}^{j}(-1)^{i}\binom{j}{i}M(k+i)}\;\ge\;0\quad\text{for all } j,k\ge0, \]

and log-convex, \(M(k)^2\le M(k-1)M(k+1)\). It decreases to \(\pi_0\). Consequently the marginal detection gain \(M(k-1)-M(k)\) of the \(k\)-th judgement is non-increasing in \(k\): detection always saturates.

\(j=1\)
\(M(k)-M(k+1)\ge0\). More agents never miss more.
\(j=2\)
\(M(k)-2M(k+1)+M(k+2)\ge0\). The gains shrink: the curve is convex.
any \(j\)
The sum equals \(\E[U^{k}(1-U)^{j}]\): the chance that a given set of \(k\) agents all miss a defect and a given set of \(j\) other agents all catch it. It is a probability, so it cannot be negative.
log-convex
\(M(k+1)/M(k)\) never decreases: each new agent removes a smaller share of the misses that remain.

Log-convexity has a simple story. The defects that survive \(k\) agents are disproportionately hard ones, so the next agent faces a harder residue than the last one did. Detection \(1-M(k)\) therefore always shows diminishing returns. That is a mathematical fact about every exchangeable population, so a saturating detection curve is evidence of nothing beyond exchangeability.

The proposition doubles as a falsification test. Measured miss curves must satisfy every one of these inequalities. A significantly negative alternating sum refutes the exchangeable-population model itself, not the estimate.

Fine print: when does the test have teeth?

If you estimate the whole curve from a single panel of \(K\) agents with the binomial formula of §8, every alternating sum of order up to \(K\) is automatically an observed frequency, so it cannot come out negative. The alternating sums bite when points on the curve come from separate experiments, from hand-picked rosters, or from teams whose members influence each other.

For a finite list of values these inequalities are necessary but not sufficient. Log-convexity and the exact moment test can fail even on single-panel data, for example when agents are anti-correlated (on every defect, exactly one of two agents flags). The figure's third check runs the exact test: it searches for a population that reproduces your values. Figure 15 runs a version of this test on your own counts that allows for sampling noise.

Check yourself
Someone reports team miss rates 0.40, 0.30 and 0.10 for teams of 1, 2 and 3 random agents. Plausible?
No. Take \(j=2\), \(k=1\): \(M(1)-2M(2)+M(3)\) \(=0.40-0.60+0.10\) \(=-0.10\), a negative "probability". The second agent catches 0.10 of new defects and the third would catch 0.20, but gains can never grow.
Detection rises from 60% to 80% to 87% as you add agents. Does that saturation show bigger teams are wasteful?
No. Every exchangeable population saturates in detection. Whether bigger teams pay depends on the horizon, which follows \(1/M(k)\) and can accelerate (next sections).

The horizon law

§4.2Theorem 4.3Eqs. (5)–(6)
Theorem 4.3 Horizon law

Under (A1)–(A3),

\[ \Pr[\text{final artifact defective}]\;\ge\;1-\bigl(1-\class{t-1}{\beta}\,\class{t-2}{(M(k)-\bar\phi)}\bigr)^{\class{t-3}{n}}, \](5)
\(\beta\)
Chance that a stage introduces a defect.
\(M(k)-\bar\phi\)
The least possible chance that the defect goes unreported (Theorem 4.1 plus the false-alarm budget).
\(\beta(M(k)-\bar\phi)\)
The per-stage leak: a defect gets in and is never reported.
\((1-\text{leak})^{n}\)
No stage out of \(n\) leaks. A leak survives to the end (A1 and disjoint components), so this is the best possible success rate.

so keeping the risk at \(\varepsilon\) requires

\[ n\;\le\;n^{*}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\class{t-1}{\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}}{\class{t-2}{\ln\frac{1}{1-\beta(M(k)-\bar\phi)}}} \;=\;\class{t-3}{\Theta\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta\,M(k)}\Bigr)}. \](6)
\(\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\)
About \(\varepsilon\) when \(\varepsilon\) is small (\(0.0513\) for \(\varepsilon=0.05\)).
\(\ln\frac{1}{1-p}\)
About the leak \(p\) when it is small.
\(\varepsilon/(\beta M(k))\)
The horizon to first order: risk budget divided by per-stage leak.

Conversely, sound agents with the "flag if anyone flags" rule and reliable repair achieve \(\Pr[\text{defective}]=1-(1-\beta M(k))^{n}\), so (6) is tight up to the false-alarm slack \(\bar\phi\).

Every stage is a small lottery: with probability \(\beta(M(k)-\bar\phi)\) a defect gets in and is never reported. A leaked defect is never revised (A1), and no later stage overwrites it (disjoint components), so it survives to the end. Surviving \(n\) stages without a leak has probability \((1-\text{leak})^n\). Set that equal to \(1-\varepsilon\), solve for \(n\), and you get the horizon.

The two-sided statement is what makes (6) a law rather than a bound: reachable depth is \(\varepsilon/(\beta M(k))\) up to constants, for every protocol. Halve the miss rate and you double the safe depth.

Worked example\(\beta=0.1\), \(\varepsilon=0.05\), \(M(k)=0.01\): leak \(=0.001\) per stage, so \(n^*\approx0.0513/0.0010005\approx51\) stages. Cut the miss rate to \(0.001\) and the horizon is about 513.

Each assumption earns its place. Without A1, an unreported defect could be rewritten away by chance. Without A2, more than \(k\) judgements could touch a component (Section 4.4). Without A3, a protocol could flag everything and never accept, which trivially catches every defect.

Check yourself
\(\beta=0.05\), \(\varepsilon=0.10\), \(M(k)=0.02\). Roughly how deep can the pipeline go?

Why does a defect that slipped past review at stage 3 still matter at stage 50?

Scaling regimes and the transition at \(a=1\)

§4.2Corollary 4.4
Corollary 4.4 Scaling regimes

Let \(\varepsilon,\beta\) be fixed.

  1. (i) If \(\pi_0>0\) then \(n^{*}(\varepsilon)\le\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\big/\ln\frac{1}{1-\beta\pi_0}\) for every \(k\): the horizon is bounded no matter how many agents verify, how often, or how cleverly.
  2. (ii) If \(\Pr[\Delta\le t]=(c+o(1))\,t^{a}\) as \(t\downarrow0\) with \(a>0\), then \(M(k)\sim c\,\Gamma(a{+}1)\,k^{-a}\) and \(n^{*}=\Theta(k^{a})\); equivalently \(a(k)\to a\).
  3. (iii) The number of agent judgements needed for a depth-\(n\) pipeline is \(J(n)=nk=\Theta\bigl(n^{1+1/a}\bigr)\): super-linear in the horizon.
  4. (iv) \(n^{*}(k)\) is convex increasing in \(k\) iff \(a>1\). Marginal returns to team size increase above the threshold and diminish below it, even though detection \(1-M(k)\) is concave in \(k\) throughout.

(i) A blind spot is a hard ceiling

\(M(k)\ge\pi_0\) for every \(k\), so every stage leaks with probability at least \(\beta\pi_0\), and the horizon is at most about \(\varepsilon/(\beta\pi_0)\). With \(\pi_0=1\%\), \(\beta=0.1\) and \(\varepsilon=0.05\), no team of any size, protocol or budget gets past roughly 51 stages.

(ii) Power-law tails give power-law horizons

\[ \class{t-1}{\Pr[\Delta\le t]\approx c\,t^{a}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{t-2}{M(k)\sim c\,\Gamma(a{+}1)\,k^{-a}},\qquad \class{t-3}{a(k)\to a} \]
\(\Pr[\Delta\le t]\approx c\,t^{a}\)
The share of defects that are at most \(t\)-visible, for small \(t\). A larger \(a\) means fewer near-invisible defects.
\(c\,\Gamma(a{+}1)\,k^{-a}\)
\(k\) agents miss roughly the defects with \(\Delta\lesssim1/k\), and there are about \(c\,k^{-a}\) of them. \(\Gamma(a+1)\) is the exact constant (it comes from \(\int_0^\infty e^{-kt}\,d(ct^{a})\)).
\(a(k)\to a\)
Doubling \(k\) divides \(k^{-a}\) by \(2^{a}\), so the doubling exponent settles at the tail exponent.

This is why the paper calls \(a\) the coverage exponent: it is simultaneously the shape of the hardest-defect tail, the slope of the miss curve on log–log axes, and the growth rate of the horizon, \(n^*\propto k^{a}\).

(iii) Verification cost grows faster than depth

To reach depth \(n\) you need \(M(k)\approx\varepsilon/(\beta n)\), so \(k\propto n^{1/a}\) agents per stage, over \(n\) stages: \(J(n)=nk\propto n^{1+1/a}\). With \(a=1\), cost grows like \(n^2\); with \(a=2\), like \(n^{1.5}\); with \(a=0.5\), like \(n^3\).

(iv) The reversal

\(n^*\propto k^{a}\) has second derivative proportional to \(a(a-1)k^{a-2}\), which is positive exactly when \(a>1\). Above the threshold each added agent buys more horizon than the one before, even while detection levels off. Below it, horizon gains shrink too.

This is the practically important reversal. Every measurement of detection shows diminishing returns, which is often read as evidence that large verifying teams are wasteful. For horizon the same data can mean the opposite.

Caveat: false alarms

Theorem 4.3 is driven by \(\beta(M(k)-\bar\phi)\), which is vacuous once \(\bar\phi\gtrsim M(k)\): a protocol allowed to flag clean components about as often as it misses defective ones satisfies it for free. \(M(k)\) falls with \(k\) while \(\bar\phi\) does not, so this is exactly the large-\(k\) regime where \(k^{a}\) scaling lives. Growing the team is a prescription conditional on keeping false alarms below the miss rate being bought. Section 4.5 turns that into a bound on team size, and the paper's experiments show it can fail outright: with a scope-limited repairer, the best team size is \(k^\dagger=1\).

When to stop adding agents

Real agents raise false alarms, and repairs damage what they touch. Let a repair fix a reported defect with probability \(\rho\), and damage a wrongly reported (clean) component with probability \(\beta_r\). The per-stage risk becomes

\[ \kappa(k)=\class{t-1}{\beta(1-\rho)}+\class{t-2}{\beta\rho\,M_Q(k)}+\class{t-3}{(1-\beta)\beta_r\bigl(1-M_P(k)\bigr)}. \](8)
\(\beta(1-\rho)\)
A defect arrives and the repair would fail even if reported. A floor that no amount of verification removes.
\(\beta\rho\,M_Q(k)\)
A defect arrives, the repair would have worked, but all \(k\) agents miss it.
\((1-\beta)\beta_r(1-M_P(k))\)
The component is clean, at least one agent raises a false alarm, and the repair breaks it.
\(M_P(k)\)
\(\E[(1-P)^k]\): the chance none of \(k\) agents false-alarms on a clean component.

The risk is at least \(\beta(1-\rho)\) for every \(k\): unreliable repair floors the risk however good the verification. The miss term falls with \(k\); the damage term rises with \(k\), because more agents give more chances for someone to cry wolf. With \(\nabla M(k)=M(k-1)-M(k)\ge0\), adding the \(k\)-th judgement lowers the risk exactly when

\[ \class{t-1}{\frac{\nabla M_Q(k)}{\nabla M_P(k)}}\;>\;\class{t-2}{\tau:=\frac{(1-\beta)\beta_r}{\beta\rho}}. \](9)
\(\nabla M_Q(k)\)
Extra defects caught by the \(k\)-th agent.
\(\nabla M_P(k)\)
Extra clean components newly flagged by the \(k\)-th agent.
\(\tau\)
The exchange rate: how many extra catches each extra false alarm must buy to break even. Large when defects are rare (small \(\beta\)) or repairs are destructive (large \(\beta_r\)).

So \(k^{\dagger}\), the last \(k\) at which the ratio exceeds \(\tau\), is where a team should stop. It is not enough that \(\nabla M_Q(k)\to0\): \(M_P\) is a moment sequence too, so its increments vanish as well. Whether a finite optimum exists turns on which vanishes faster, that is, on whether false alarms are less correlated across agents than misses.

The natural sufficient condition is that false alarms are nearly independent. Then \(1-M_P(k)\approx k\,\E[P]\) and \(\nabla M_P(k)\approx\E[P]\) over the operating range \(k\ll1/\E[P]\), while \(\nabla M_Q\) is non-increasing by complete monotonicity. The ratio crosses \(\tau\) once, so \(k^\dagger\) exists and minimises \(\kappa\). With \(M_Q(k)\asymp Ck^{-a}\), \(\nabla M_Q\) falls like \(aCk^{-(a+1)}\), and

\[ k^{\dagger}\approx\Bigl(\frac{\class{t-1}{aC}}{\class{t-2}{\tau\,\E[P]}}\Bigr)^{\class{t-3}{1/(a+1)}}. \](10)
\(aC\,k^{-(a+1)}\)
The \(k\)-th agent's marginal catch: the derivative of \(Ck^{-a}\).
\(\tau\,\E[P]\)
What its marginal false alarms cost, in units of catches.
\(1/(a+1)\)
Solving \(aCk^{-(a+1)}=\tau\E[P]\) for \(k\).

Two degenerate cases bracket this. If \(M_P=M_Q\) (agents with no discriminative power), the ratio is identically 1 and \(k^\dagger\) is 0 or \(\infty\) as \(\tau\) is above or below 1. If \(M_Q\) decays more slowly than \(M_P\), the ratio diverges, every large team pays for itself, and \(k^\dagger=\infty\). Beyond the operating range the linearisation fails and false alarms saturate, so (10) locates an optimum for the team sizes one would field, not a global one.

How many agents are worth putting on verification is thus as much a property of the repairer, and of how independent false alarms are, as of the verifiers.

Measuring the coverage exponent

One calibration set estimates the miss curve. How precise is it, and what does it say about teams larger than the one you measured?

Calibrating a population

§5§8Eq. (11)

Calibration is easy in principle. Show \(N\) defects with known ground truth to \(k\) agents and record how many flag each. That estimates \(M(1),\dots,M(k)\) and certifies the horizon for teams up to size \(k\). Two things then need quantifying: how precisely \(N\) defects pin down the exponent, and what the data say about larger teams.

The estimator

Let \(f_i\) be the number of the \(k\) agents that flagged defect \(i\). The paper's estimator (stated in §8) is

\[ \widehat M(j)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\class{t-1}{\binom{k-f_i}{j}\Big/\binom{k}{j}}. \]
\(\binom{k-f_i}{j}\big/\binom{k}{j}\)
For defect \(i\): the chance that \(j\) agents picked at random from the \(k\) all missed it (all \(j\) come from the \(k-f_i\) who did not flag).

Averaging over defects gives the miss curve of a \(j\)-agent team exactly, with no model assumed. It is unbiased for \(M(j)\) for every \(j\le k\).

Error bars on the exponent

\(\widehat M(j)\) is a sample mean of \(U^{j}\), so the delta method applied to \(\hat a(j)=\log_2\widehat M(j)/\widehat M(2j)\) gives

\[ \operatorname{se}(\hat a(j))=\class{t-1}{\frac{1}{\sqrt N\,\ln 2}} \Bigl[\class{t-2}{\tfrac{V_j}{M(j)^2}}+\class{t-3}{\tfrac{V_{2j}}{M(2j)^2}} -\class{t-4}{\tfrac{2C_j}{M(j)M(2j)}}\Bigr]^{1/2}, \](11)

with \(V_j=M(2j)-M(j)^2\), \(V_{2j}=M(4j)-M(2j)^2\), \(C_j=M(3j)-M(j)M(2j)\).

\(1/(\sqrt N\ln2)\)
Averaging over \(N\) defects, converted to \(\log_2\) units.
\(V_j/M(j)^2\)
Squared relative noise of one defect's contribution to \(\widehat M(j)\). \(V_j=\operatorname{Var}(U^j)\) because \(\E[U^{2j}]=M(2j)\).
\(V_{2j}/M(2j)^2\)
The same for \(\widehat M(2j)\). It dominates, because \(M(2j)\) is the smaller number.
\(2C_j/(M(j)M(2j))\)
\(U^{j}\) and \(U^{2j}\) move together across defects, which partly cancels in their ratio. \(C_j=\operatorname{Cov}(U^j,U^{2j})\).

Every one of these moments comes from the same calibration set. The bracket blows up as \(M(2j)\) approaches the resolution of the sample, so the binding requirement is \(N\,M(2j)\gg1\): the set must contain many defects that a \(2j\)-agent team would still miss. A rule of thumb from §8: resolving whether \(\hat a(k)\) is above or below 1 to \(\pm0.1\) needs on the order of \(10^{2}/M(2k)\) labelled defects. Cheap while \(M(2k)\) is a few percent, prohibitive once the team is good.

Precision vs reach\(N\) (more defects) buys precision. \(k\) (more agents per defect) buys reach: how far along the curve you can see. One cannot be bought with the other.

Fine print: when Eq. (11) is exact

The formula treats each defect's miss probability \(U\) as known. That holds in the paper's testbeds, where the panel is the whole finite pool of agents. When each defect is judged by only \(K\) randomly drawn agents, the panel's own binomial noise adds to the error. The lab below reports both: Eq. (11), and a data-based version of the same delta method that includes panel noise. The two agree as \(K\) grows.

What calibration can never tell you

\[ \Bigl[\class{t-1}{\min,\max}\Bigr]\; \class{t-2}{\E_{w}[U^{k'}]}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \class{t-3}{\E_w[U^{k}]=M(k)\ (k\le K)},\ \ \class{t-4}{w\in\cM_1([0,1])}, \](12)

This is the truncated Hausdorff moment problem. Infinitely many laws on \([0,1]\) share the same first \(K\) moments, and their later moments sweep out an interval. The interval is sharp: every value inside it is the \(M(k')\) of some population consistent with the data. \(w(\{1\})\) is the mass at \(U=1\), which is \(\Delta=0\): the blind spot. Corollary 5.2 A blind-spot floor cannot be certified

Fix \(K\) and \(M(1),\dots,M(K)\) in the interior of the moment cone. The identified set always contains a population with \(\pi_0=0\). Finite calibration therefore cannot certify \(\pi_0>0\), and so cannot establish that more agents would fail to help; nor does it license a point prediction of \(M(k')\) for \(k'>K\), whose identified interval has positive width. It does license the conservative direction: the upper endpoint of (12) bounds \(M(k')\) and hence lower-bounds the horizon a team of size \(k'\) attains.

The two halves are not in tension. Bad news, an irreducible blind spot, is unfalsifiable from finite data; good news is certifiable. Some population with \(\pi_0=0\) always fits the data, so you can never prove more agents will not help. But \(n^*\propto1/M\) is monotone and a guarantee needs only one endpoint: the upper end of the interval bounds \(M(k')\) from above and so bounds the horizon from below.

Neither half licenses a point extrapolation. A fitted parametric tail (the log–log slope of \(M\), a Beta mixture, a species-richness estimator) lands somewhere inside the interval and inherits no guarantee from it.

Choosing who verifies

With a fixed pool of distinct agents, which \(k\) should check the work? Choose for coverage, and know which guarantees survive.

Choose for coverage

§6Proposition 6.1Proposition 6.2

\[ M(S)=\E_\theta\class{t-1}{\prod_{i\in S}(1-\Delta_i(\theta))},\qquad \class{t-2}{\Lambda(S)=\log\frac{1}{M(S)}}. \]
\(\prod_{i\in S}(1-\Delta_i(\theta))\)
Every member of team \(S\) misses defect \(\theta\).
\(\Lambda(S)\)
The team's coverage exponent. Horizon \(\propto1/M(S)=e^{\Lambda(S)}\), so \(\Lambda\) is linear in log-horizon.

Greedy maximisation of detection \(1-M(S)\) and of \(\Lambda(S)\) pick the same agent at every step, since both simply minimise \(M(S\cup\{e\})\). The algorithm is not in question; the guarantee is. Detection is monotone submodular, so greedy attains \((1-1/e)\) of the optimal detection. But that only implies

\[ M(S_{\mathrm{greedy}})\le e^{-1}+(1-e^{-1})\,M(S^{*}), \]

a horizon ratio that tends to 0 as \(M(S^*)\to0\). If the best team misses 1%, the guarantee still allows greedy to miss 37.4%, a horizon 37 times shorter. For horizon it is vacuous.

Proposition 6.1 When the exponent is submodular

If every \(q_i\) is a non-increasing function of a common scalar difficulty \(\theta\) (blind spots are nested rather than complementary), then \(\Lambda\) is monotone submodular, and greedy achieves \(\Lambda(S_{\mathrm{greedy}})\ge(1-1/e)\Lambda(S^{*})\), i.e. a horizon at least \((n^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}})^{1-1/e}\) up to constants. Without an association condition \(\Lambda\) can be supermodular and greedy arbitrarily bad.

Submodular means diminishing returns: adding agent \(e\) to a small team raises \(\Lambda\) at least as much as adding it to a larger team that contains the small one. Nested blind spots give this. If every agent's detection falls as one scalar difficulty rises, a strong agent's catches mostly include a weak agent's catches. Complementary blind spots break it. A specialist alone barely moves \(\Lambda\), but the specialist that completes a cover can send \(\Lambda\) to infinity. That is supermodular, and greedy can then be arbitrarily bad.

The condition is awkward as well as sufficient. Nested blind spots are the positively associated regime, which is also where ranking agents by individual accuracy already picks well, so the guarantee holds where selection matters least. It is weak in absolute terms too: \((n^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}})^{1-1/e}\) turns an optimal depth of 100 into a promise of 18. Where selection actually pays (complementary blind spots), \(\Lambda\) can be supermodular and no constant-factor guarantee survives.

Proposition 6.2 Accuracy ranking is \(k\) times worse

For every \(k\) and \(\eta\in(0,1)\) there is a pool of \(2k\) agents in which selecting the \(k\) individually most accurate agents yields miss rate \(\eta\) while the coverage-optimal team of the same size yields \(\eta/k\); the horizon ratio is exactly \(k\).

Part V · §7

Evidence from two executable testbeds

Correctness, defects and agent judgements are computed rather than assumed, and nothing is fitted that can be computed.

The testbeds and what they showed

§7.1§7.2§7.3
Testbed A · distributed plan verification

A team maintains an assignment to a scale-free constraint network, the standard model of agents holding partial views of a joint solution. Each auditor sees only a scope, the variables it owns, and checks exactly the requirements inside it. A few also hold a private, incorrect requirement, which produces false alarms. A defect is a single-variable slip violating exactly one requirement. Scopes come from a few shared templates, so blind spots are correlated by construction.

Testbed B · program verification

Artifacts are real Python functions; defects are single-point AST mutants whose behaviour differs from the reference on a large input pool, so "defective" is decided by execution. A verifier is a property-based tester with one sampling strategy and a few generic properties drawn from a shared vocabulary.

In each testbed, four populations differ in how much their blind spots overlap. Only their effort is tuned (scope size in A, check completion in B), so all four share one per-agent detection rate. That isolates overlap from accuracy.

Miss curves and exponents

  • At identical per-agent accuracy, Testbed A's populations reach miss rates at \(k=64\) spanning about two orders of magnitude.
  • The siloed population's \(a(k)\) stays low and decays to 0, the signature of a blind-spot atom and a direct measurement of the ceiling in Corollary 4.4(i). The diverse population has no atom, crosses \(a=1\), and keeps climbing, so past that size each doubling more than doubles the horizon.
  • Across replicates, accuracy-matched populations differ in measured horizon by about 15×.
  • A stricter control drops the matching: at identical scope size, the diverse population's agents are less accurate than the siloed population's, yet the diverse team misses less. Worse agents, longer horizon.
  • Committees are distinct agents from a finite pool, so predictions use the exact without-replacement curve. Its Hausdorff residuals are non-negative to numerical precision: the model survives the test that could have refuted it.

The horizon law in a running process

  • Each stage a planner edits one variable, slipping with probability \(\beta\); \(k\) agents inspect the change; if any flags, repair follows. Flags come from evaluating the agents' checks and repairs from running their search.
  • Success decays geometrically with depth, as Theorem 4.3 requires: \((1-\hat\kappa)^n\), with \(\hat\kappa\) estimated at depth 1 alone, predicts depths up to 64.
  • Per-stage risk tracks \(\beta M(k)\) across four populations and eight team sizes, over more than a decade of risk.
  • With the repairer restricted to its own scope, per-stage risk flattens while \(\beta M(k)\) keeps falling: the \(\beta(1-\rho)\) term of Eq. (8) takes over. Locality caps what agents can fix, not only what they see, and this second reason adding verifiers stops helping is invisible to any analysis of detection alone.

Re-review, identification, selection

  • Under full re-review the siloed population's success decays toward zero by depth 150 while the diverse one holds. Their partial sums \(\sum_{s\le n}M(ks)\) are 112.6 and 0.80: divergent against convergent, as Theorem 4.6 requires.
  • With false alarms, a competent repairer makes extra verifiers pay monotonically. A scope-limited repairer with measured damage rate \(\beta_r=0.70\) makes risk rise from the first extra verifier (\(k^\dagger=1\)). At \(\beta_r=0.15\), Eq. (10) predicts \(k^\dagger\approx9\) against an exact optimum of 8. The linearisation \(1-M_P(k)\approx k\E[P]\) holds to 7% out to \(k=16\).
  • Calibrating with \(K=8\) agents leaves \(M(64)\) wide open: for the siloed population, anywhere between 0.20 and 0.79, a fourfold uncertainty in horizon. The sharp lower bound on \(\pi_0\) is 0 everywhere, including the two populations whose true blind-spot mass exceeds 0.4. Against the trivial bound \(M(64)\le M(8)\), the LP tightens barely at all where blind spots dominate and about fivefold where they do not.
  • Ranking by individual detection rate costs a factor that grows with team size, because the most accurate agents come from one role template.

Numerical verification. Independently of the testbeds, every theorem is checked by exhaustive or randomised search over its hypotheses: the scrutiny bound against arbitrary randomised aggregators and the Neyman–Pearson-optimal rule, complete monotonicity and log-convexity, the \(k^{a}\) scaling and the \(a=1\) series criterion, the accuracy–coverage separation, submodularity of \(\Lambda\), the greedy guarantee, coverage of the identified sets, and finiteness of \(k^\dagger\). Part VI · §8–9

Using it

A measurement protocol you can run on your own agents, the scope of the claims, and how to read published results.

A measurement protocol

§8 Discussion

All of this needs one calibration set and no new instrumentation.

  1. Collect \(N\) artifacts with known defects.
  2. Have \(k\) agents from the deployment's own design distribution judge each one.
  3. Record only \(f_i\), the number that flag defect \(i\).
  4. Compute the exact miss curve of a \(j\)-agent team: \[ \widehat M(j)=\frac1N\sum_i\binom{k-f_i}{j}\Big/\binom{k}{j}. \]

Three decisions read off it:

  • Hire?Is the next agent worth adding? Check whether \(\hat a(k)\) is above or below 1.
  • Extrapolate?Is a claim about a larger team supported? Use the identified interval of Theorem 5.1.
  • Model ok?Does the population model hold at all? Check the Hausdorff residuals of Proposition 4.2.

\(N\) buys precision and \(k\) buys reach, and the second cannot be bought with the first. Since \(\operatorname{se}(\hat a)\) in (11) scales as \((N\,M(2k))^{-1/2}\), resolving \(\hat a(k)\gtrless1\) to \(\pm0.1\) needs on the order of \(10^{2}/M(2k)\) labelled defects.

Scope, and how to read reported results

§8 Discussion

Scope. The conditionally i.i.d. reduction (Lemma 3.1) is exact for teams sampled from a design distribution and approximate for a hand-picked roster. Proposition 4.2 makes it testable, and the testbeds' curves pass, but a deliberately anti-correlated team escapes it, which is the point of selection. The horizon law assumes stage-disjoint writes and flag-gated repair. The testbeds are executable by design, which is what makes validation possible, but it means the numerical \(a\) and \(\pi_0\) are properties of those populations, not of any deployed system.

Reading reported results. The paper measured no language-model team, but the theory makes published disagreements legible.

  • Reporting detection over a jury measures \(1-M(k)\), which Proposition 4.2 forces to saturate. Saturation is evidence of nothing beyond exchangeability.
  • Resampling against a fixed imperfect verifier is the \(k=1\) corner, with ceiling \(M(1)\).
  • Teams that scale well are above \(a=1\); teams that do not are below it or up against a blind-spot mass. Both can hold for one architecture on different task distributions, since \(a\) depends on the population and on the defect law \(\mu\).
What would falsify the theory

A deployment whose \(\hat a(k)\) sits well below 1, yet whose reliability improves faster than \(k^{\hat a}\) as the team grows.

Conclusion

§9

Adding an agent to a verifying team buys coverage, not opinions. Coverage is a moment curve of the population's discriminative power: it caps what any protocol achieves, converts into a horizon law with matching bounds, and crosses \(a=1\) where team growth and repeated review begin to pay off. Mean accuracy carries none of this. The lesson is a change of question: not how good are our verifiers, but what do they all fail to see.

The paper on one page

review sheet
  1. A defect has a hidden state \(\theta\). A random agent's verdict moves with probability \(\Delta(\theta)=|Q-P|\).
  2. Given \(\theta\), a random team is \(k\) coin flips. Agents fail together exactly as much as defects differ in visibility.
  3. The miss curve \(M(k)=\E[(1-\Delta)^k]\) is the \(k\)-th moment of \(1-\Delta\). Its doubling exponent \(a(k)\) is the coverage exponent; \(\pi_0\) is its floor.
  4. No protocol using \(k\) judgements gets detection minus false alarms above \(1-M(k)\). Debate reshuffles judgements; it does not create them.
  5. Every miss curve is completely monotone and log-convex, so detection always saturates. Measured curves that violate this refute the model.
  6. Safe depth is \(n^*\approx\varepsilon/(\beta M(k))\), and that is tight.
  7. A blind spot caps depth at about \(\varepsilon/(\beta\pi_0)\). A power tail gives \(n^*\propto k^{a}\) and cost \(\propto n^{1+1/a}\). Above \(a=1\), each agent buys more horizon than the last.
  8. Per-agent accuracy says nothing about \(a\): any accuracy is compatible with any exponent.
  9. Re-reviewing everything with fresh teams keeps a pipeline clean forever iff \(\sum M(ks)\lt\infty\), i.e. \(a>1\). Team size cannot change this; fixed rosters never help.
  10. False alarms and damaging repairs make a finite team size optimal: stop when \(\nabla M_Q/\nabla M_P\) falls below \(\tau\).
  11. \(N\) defects buy precision, \(k\) agents buy reach. You need \(N\,M(2k)\gg1\).
  12. Calibrating with \(K\) agents identifies larger teams only up to an interval. A blind spot can never be certified; a horizon lower bound can.
  13. Choose teams for coverage \(\Lambda(S)\). Greedy's \((1-1/e)\) guarantee is vacuous for horizon, and accuracy ranking can be \(k\) times worse.
